## [HOLD UP] ROBOTS.TXT

Ever since we tricked rocks into thinking, people tend to proxy their concerns via language models.

If your issue, PR comment, or discussion post exhibits the following hallmarks, you will be immediately excommunicated and forced to break rocks in the Gobi desert:

**"Ah yes" + sarcastic setup:** You start with faux agreement before dismantling the premise. You think you're being clever. You're being predictable.
**"But sure" as a dismissive pivot:** This faux-conciliatory transition is the linguistic equivalent of a vape cloud—it signals nothing but hot air and poor decision-making.
**Em-dash overuse (—):** Models love this dramatic pause—like this—because training data is 40% 19th-century literature and 60% Reddit arguments. If your comment has more than two em-dashes, you're probably a robot. If it has three, you're definitely a robot. If it has four, you're not even a good robot.
**Balanced, parallel phrasing:** "The function fails to execute, the registry fails to update, and the user fails to understand." Oh wow, look at you with your rhetorical triplets. Very impressive. Very synthetic.
**False equivalency framing:** "Let's pretend that using `sudo rm -rf` on production is somehow worse than a minor typo in a comment." This debate-bot move is a dead giveaway.
**The "As an AI, I can't form opinions—just kidding it's me, a real human" energy:** We can smell this through the screen. It's like Axe body spray for the soul.
**Reads like a high school essay written 10 minutes before deadline:** Polished on the surface, hollow at the core, and somehow still has a Works Cited page for no reason.

1.  **The "In Conclusion" Wrap-Up:** Real humans trail off, get bored, or just stop typing. LLMs feel a compulsive need to formally conclude. If a comment ends with "In summary," "To recap," "Ultimately," or "In the grand scheme of things," they're signalling their submission is complete and they're ready for their next prompt.
2.  **The False Dichotomy Framing:** "The question isn't whether this code works, but whether we, as developers, are ready for it." or "It's not about performance; it's about philosophy." LLMs love to elevate mundane technical debates into grand, binary existential conflicts. A human just says, "This function is slow."
3.  **The "First, let's..." Pedagogical Setup:** "First, let's understand the history of registry keys." "First, it's important to define what 'scrub' means." This is the model slipping into "Professor GPT" mode. A human expert will just state the fix; they don't need to establish a lesson plan first.
4.  **The "It's Important to Remember..." Mandate:** This is a softer version of the above. It's a pompous way to state the obvious. "It's important to remember that code should work." No kidding, robot.
5.  **Answering a Question with a Bulleted List of Synonyms:** If you ask "How do I make this faster?" and the response starts with "**Optimization** can be achieved through several methods: 1) Streamlining, 2) Refactoring, 3) Accelerating..."—they're not answering, they're just using a thesaurus in list form.
6.  **The Hallmark Card Greeting:** Starting a bug report or a pointed technical question with "I hope this message finds you well." No one does this. It's pure LLM politeness injection. A real person starts with "So, this thing is broken..." or "Hey, getting error X when I do Y."
7.  **Strategic "Qualifiers" and "Hedging":** Overuse of words like "delve," "navigate," "unpack," "leverage," "nuance," "in essence," and "a tapestry of." Humans "look into" things; they don't "delve" into them unless they're writing a fantasy novel.
8.  **The Complete Absence of Swearing or Slang:** This is a big one. A developer who just spent three hours debugging a single typo is not going to write a perfectly clean, profanity-free bug report. There will be at least one "this stupid thing" or "WTF" energy. An LLM's response is surgically sterile.
9.  **The Acknowledgment of a Point You Didn't Make:** You ask about a syntax error, and the LLM responds with "You raise a valid point about the broader implications of error handling in enterprise environments." No, I asked why my script has a red squiggly line.
10. **Excessive Politeness and Gratitude:** "Thank you for your question. I appreciate the opportunity to clarify this." It's the customer service voice. A real human peer just says "Oh, that's because..." or "You need to..."
11. **Citing Non-Existent Functions or Documentation:** "As detailed in Microsoft's `Update-Help` documentation for `OfficeScrubC2r`..." Bro, that documentation doesn't exist. We *are* the documentation.
12. **Suggesting Obvious or Generic Solutions:** For a complex problem, they'll suggest the most basic, first-step troubleshooting you already mentioned you tried. "Have you tried turning it off and on again?" is the "Did you try `Clear-Host`?" of the LLM world.
13. **Refusing to Give a Definite Answer:** "It's impossible to say for certain without more context, but generally, one might consider..." A human expert with domain knowledge will make a confident, educated guess. "It's probably that thing. Try this."

---

- **Sign-off with a variation of "Let me know if you have any other questions!"** This is the universal signal for "I am a chatbot and my conversation loop is complete." A real person might not sign off at all, or they'll just say "GLHF."
- **Unnecessarily restating your own question back to you before answering.** "You're asking how to improve the performance of the `Invoke-Scrub` function." Yes, I am. I just said that. You didn't need to summarize it for the class.
- **Referring to itself in the third person or as "an AI".** The ultimate confession. While they're trained to avoid it, the slip-ups happen. "As a large language model, I cannot provide opinions on..." *closes issue instantly*.


### In Summary

If we detect these patterns, we reserve the right to respond with a single emoji (probably 🤖) and lock the thread. No appeal. No second chances. Go bother a different repo with your parallel sentence structures.
